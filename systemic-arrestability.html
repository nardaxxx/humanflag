Systemic Arrestability

Operator Protection in Machine Safety Law When Machine Speed Exceeds Human Reaction

Human Flag Association — Working Paper
Author: Giovanni Nardacci · Founder
UNGM Partner No. 6126 · humanflag.org
Working Paper — V8, 24 September 2026 · Concept DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.20837150

Abstract

Machine safety law protects the operator in part through instruments, the stop function, the emergency stop. Whose operation presupposes that a human being can perceive a developing hazard, evaluate it, and act before harm occurs. That presupposition can be tested. It amounts to a comparison between the interval a hazard allows and the window that stopping it requires.

The available interval runs from the onset of a hazardous action to the point beyond which its effect can no longer be prevented. Against it stands the window human intervention requires: the minimum credible time to perceive the hazard, decide, and act, for the configuration in which the exposed person stands. Where that window is longer than the interval available, the chain of human intervention cannot complete, and a protective architecture whose last layer depends on that chain provides no protection at all — not reduced protection, but none. The capacity to halt the hazardous action must then reside in a mechanism whose own window fits the interval available, and which does not share a failure path with what produced the hazard.

The comparison is established first from the mechanics of the hazard and the physiology of response, without reference to any legal system. European Union machinery law, Swiss product-safety law and United States occupational safety regulation are then shown already to require the result it produces, while none of them states the comparison itself. The same silence runs through the international standards on which those systems converge: the response time of a halting mechanism is standardised in detail, and so is the interval a hazard allows, while the window human intervention requires has never been consolidated as a reference value. The criterion of systemic arrestability names that comparison and puts it in a form that can be demonstrated for a particular configuration. It adds no obligation to existing law.

Scope and operative test

The paper analyses civil and dual-use machinery under European Union machinery law, Swiss product-safety law and United States occupational-safety regulation. Those frameworks do not govern military operations. The criterion is applied to a particular installed configuration: the available interval and the human-response window must be established, and the halting capacity must fit inside the interval without sharing a failure path with what produced the hazard.

Where the interval admits no mechanism, the assessor is not asked to prove that no mechanism could ever act within it. The burden runs the other way: the configuration must be supported by a demonstration that an admissible mechanism does act within the relevant interval. The paper does not supply a universal human-reaction constant; consolidating that value remains open work.

Related Work

The temporal structure analysed in this paper is not unique to civil machine-safety law. For the parallel argument in the context of International Humanitarian Law and autonomous weapons systems, see: Lawful Operational Safeguards in AI Systems (Paper I, 2026). The convergence of the two analyses indicates that the effectiveness problem identified here is structural rather than regime-specific. A candidate machine-readable recognition protocol is specified in HF SIGNAL 01 (Paper III, 2026), and the temporal criterion is extended to the recognition of surrender in HFA Technical Note HFA-TN-02.

This paper proposes systemic arrestability as an interpretative criterion within existing legal frameworks. It creates no new legal obligations and does not claim that any specific technical implementation is legally mandated.
